You're right that it heats evenly, but the main reason is heat transport, not scattering.

**What scattering and clouds do:** they make the light even. Soft, shadowless, diffuse brightness, with much of the sunlight reflected back off the high cloud tops. That matters for diffuse skin light-sensing and the overall look. But scattering mostly sends light up or down, not sideways. The equator still takes in more energy than the poles at the top of the atmosphere.

**What moves the heat:** a dense atmosphere holds a lot of heat and carries it poleward in large circulation loops. Your setup favors this twice:
- **Dense air** holds more heat per volume, so winds move far more of it.
- **Slow rotation.** The 11-day cycle means the moon spins slowly, so the sideways force that breaks Earth's circulation into three bands per hemisphere is weak. On a slow rotator the loops can stretch from equator to pole. Venus (dense, very slow) has almost no temperature difference from equator to pole, or even from day side to night side. Titan (dense, 16-day cycle) differs by only a few degrees.

So "the moon heats fairly evenly" holds up well.

**Where the "equator" is:** the moon is tidally locked and orbits the planet's equator, and the planet is tilted 30°. So the moon's spin axis is probably tilted about 30° relative to the sun. That means a tropical band roughly ±30° wide, with seasons running on the ~4-year planet orbit. Thick air would soften them a lot.

**Two corrections to my last message:**
- **Mountains are a weaker barrier than I said.** A moon has lower gravity, so its atmosphere thins out more slowly with height. Passes would still be colder and drier than the lowlands, but not deadly the way I implied. The range is more of a strain than a wall.
- **The dry spots** are where the big loops sink back down. On Earth that's about 30° latitude, where the deserts are. With pole-spanning loops it would be near the poles, and with this much moisture it would be "less wet," not desert. That fits your no-desert call.

This rests on two assumptions: the moon's spin axis lines up with its orbit (normal for tidal locking), and the 11-day cycle is its rotation period.

Yes, you need pores, or at least some intake openings, for two reasons.

**1. Skin diffusion can't feed a humanoid-sized, active body.** Oxygen only penetrates about a millimeter into tissue by diffusion alone. Below that it has to be carried. Even with blood vessels right under the skin, the limit is surface area versus body volume:
- An adult human has about 2 m² of skin but about 70 m² of lung surface. Skin breathing supplies only about 1–2% of a human's oxygen.
- Animals that breathe only through skin, like lungless salamanders, are small, thin and slow. The Lake Titicaca frog manages by growing baggy skin folds for extra area.
- Your dense atmosphere helps. Diffusion scales with oxygen pressure, so roughly twice Earth's pressure means roughly twice the uptake. But that's 2×, not the 35× the gap would need.
- Wall-less cells make the skin thinner and easier to cross, but thickness isn't the bottleneck. Area and distance are.

So skin-only would mean a sluggish creature with low metabolism and heavy folds or frills, which doesn't match active humanoids that fight and talk.

**2. Your speech and joy mechanism already needs air inside the body.** The mouth vents pressurized internal gas, and the giggle is overflow from that store. Something has to let bulk air in, and diffusion through skin can't move volume. Pores are the intake side of the pump.

**A workable split:** the outer layer, a few millimeters deep, breathes directly through the skin. That fits an existing [C] that cartilage-like tissue gets by on diffusion.
