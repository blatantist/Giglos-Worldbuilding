## Turn 1 - Assistant (Claude)
<!-- 2026-10-07T15:18:39.934235Z -->

This is a much better fit for Jev than text generation. Choosing the right option from a labeled list given a state is exactly what it was trained for, so you're not fighting its training objective like the token-walk scheme does.

There are three places it could sit, from least to most invasive:

- **Goal selection:** the blackboard goes in as state, the active goals go in as options. This is the slowest and most semantic decision in GOAP, and normally the most hand-tuned.
- **Action choice at each plan step:** the options are only the actions whose preconditions are currently satisfied, which keeps the list small and well under the 255 cap. Put preconditions and effects in each option's description, since jevchat found full-text options roughly tripled accuracy over bare symbols.
- **Soft costs inside the planner:** turn Jev's calibrated probabilities into edge costs (something like -log p) so A* still does the search but Jev supplies the judgment about which actions fit. That keeps the plans symbolically valid and interpretable while the "taste" is learned rather than handwritten.

Latency is the main constraint. Based on jevgpt's numbers, a call is around a second or two and a fraction of a cent, so Jev can't run per tick. It belongs on the slow timescale (goal arbitration, replan triggers) while the fast layers stay plain GOAP/FSM. The probability distribution is also a free signal: a flat distribution means "I'm ambiguous here," which could escalate to a slower layer or trigger a replan through the blackboard.

Where were you picturing it? Replacing the hand-written goal scorer is the cleanest first experiment, because you can A/B it against the existing scorer on the same blackboard states.

## Turn 3 - Assistant (Claude)
<!-- 2026-10-07T15:24:39.792273Z -->

That's a good case, because it exposes what a scalar score can't represent: the key option has a cheap-looking plan but an unknown cost, while the axe option has a known cost spread over a longer chain. A normal GOAP scorer has to flatten both into one number, so the uncertainty gets lost or hardcoded as a guess. Jev can reason about it semantically, if you give it the right material.

**State (shared across options):**
- The goal and the blocker (door, its two valid unlock conditions)
- Inventory, nearby known resources (tree, tools), and what the agent has already searched
- Anything it believes about the key's location, even weak priors ("keys tend to be on the door's owner or in chests")

**Options (what differs per choice):** each branch rendered as its full plan chain, with the planner's knowledge tagged on it.
- "Axe: go to tree, chop, craft, return. Cost known, 4 steps, all preconditions satisfied or reachable."
- "Key: location unknown, 1 precondition, cost unbounded, 2 unexplored rooms, 1 chest."
- Side effects belong here too: the axe is reusable, but breaking the door might be permanent or loud.

The planner stays in charge of the parts it's good at, which are the known costs and the chain of preconditions. Jev handles what it can't: the unknown-cost branch, side effects, and commonsense priors. A flat distribution between the two is also useful, since it says the choice is close and cheap exploration might settle it.

One thing worth designing early is re-decision. "Search for the key" has no natural end, so you'd want triggers, like a step budget or a failed room, that put Jev back at the same decision with the updated state ("searched 3 rooms, no key"). Otherwise it commits forever to the first pick, which is the same failure a pure scorer has.

For testing, build a handful of scenarios like this where the right answer is obvious to you, and compare Jev against your hand scorer on them. Whether Jev's judgment on unknown-cost tradeoffs is actually calibrated is an open question, and it's cheap to check.

## Turn 5 - Assistant (Claude)
<!-- 2026-10-07T15:27:12.406338Z -->

That works, and it removes the commitment problem I flagged. Per-step re-decision means "give up on the key" is just another outcome of the same choice, with no special trigger needed. Two things make it work well:

- **Give it the history.** "Searched 3 rooms, 40 steps spent on the key branch" has to be in the state, otherwise each call sees a fresh world and has no reason to give up. The current target should also appear as an explicit "continue" option showing what it has cost so far, so abandoning it is a visible tradeoff.
- **Watch for thrashing.** If the two branches score close, Jev could flip each step, walking toward the tree, then back toward the rooms, and never finishing either. A switch needs enough of an advantage to overcome the sunk travel cost. You can get that by tagging switch options with "abandons N steps of progress" in their description, or by requiring the new choice to win by a margin in probability. This is hysteresis, and your flat-distribution signal can drive it.

For latency, you probably don't want a call on every tick, just at decision points: a sub-step finishes, a room turns up empty, or something new is observed. Between those, plain GOAP execution carries the agent along.

One more thought: your giving-up behavior is basically explore/exploit. A scorer would need a hand-tuned patience parameter per scenario, while Jev can read the situation ("a locked house, likely no key nearby") and judge when exploring has stopped paying off. That's probably the strongest case for using it here.
