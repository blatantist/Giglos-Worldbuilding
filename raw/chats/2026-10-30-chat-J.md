**The planet's shadow is small.** It's a narrow cone pointing straight away from the sun. A moon only crosses it for a short stretch of each orbit: a few hours at most for a moon like ours. It never lasts a month. Even a big planet with a close moon would only black it out for hours, maybe up to about a day.

**The long dark you're thinking of is probably ordinary night.** Big moons are usually tidally locked, so the same face always points at the planet. That makes the moon's day as long as its orbit. A moon that orbits every 30 days gets about 15 days of sunlight and then about 15 days of night on any given spot. That's just the moon turning, not the planet blocking the sun.

**Your sideways orbit works, with one catch.** The orbit's tilt stays fixed as the planet goes around the sun. So the orbit only faces the sun head-on at two points in the year:

- **Facing the sun:** the moon never passes behind the planet, so no eclipses at all for a long stretch.
- **A quarter-year later:** the orbit is edge-on to the sun, and the moon crosses the shadow once every orbit. That gives an "eclipse season," twice a year.

Uranus is the real example. It's tipped on its side and its moons orbit around its equator. For decades at a time they never pass into its shadow, and around its equinoxes they get eclipsed every orbit.

Getting no eclipses all year round would need the orbit to swivel exactly once per year to keep facing the sun. Satellites around Earth do this on purpose, using the pull of Earth's slightly squashed shape. For a natural moon it would be a big coincidence, but it's fine as a fictional handwave.

**What this means for Giglos:** the planet blocking the sun isn't a problem. The setting choices worth making are:

1. Is Giglos tidally locked? If so, the orbit length sets how long day and night are.
2. Do you want eclipse seasons? They could be a nice cultural or religious hook, either rare and dramatic or regular and expected.
3. How tilted is the moon's orbit compared to the planet's path around the sun?

|---|---|---|---|

**What keeps a long night warm:**

- **Thick air.** Denser air holds more heat and carries it around the globe. Something like 2–3× Earth's pressure would soften a week-long night a lot.
- **Oceans.** Water stores a huge amount of heat and releases it slowly. Earth's coasts swing much less than its deserts.
- **Wind.** On a tidally locked moon the sunlit side heats the air, which then flows to the dark side. The cost is strong, steady winds along the line between day and night.
- **Clouds and greenhouse gases.** These act like a blanket, so night-time clouds trap heat.
- **Heat from the planet's pull (tidal heating).** The planet squeezes the moon as it orbits, especially if the orbit is a bit oval. That heats the inside, the way Jupiter keeps Io volcanic and Europa's ocean liquid. A close orbit gives more of this heat.
- **Warmth from the planet.** On the side facing the planet, sunlight bouncing off it brightens the night. A gas giant also gives off some heat of its own.

**This pushes toward a closer orbit.** A close orbit gives shorter nights, more heat from squeezing, and a bigger, brighter planet in the near-side sky. ## How clouds form

1. **Air rises.** Sun-warmed ground heats the air, or wind pushes it up a slope, or a cold air mass slides under it.
2. **It cools as it rises** and eventually can't hold all its water vapor.
3. **The vapor condenses into droplets, but only onto a surface.** Pure vapor almost never forms droplets on its own; it would take enormous oversaturation. Each droplet starts on a tiny airborne particle: sea salt, dust, smoke, pollen, bacteria, spores. These are called cloud condensation nuclei.

So particles aren't optional. Essentially every cloud droplet on Earth formed on one. They're so common, though, that the limit on clouds is almost always moisture and rising air, not particles. You're right that clouds would form on Giglos anyway.

**What particles do control is what kind of cloud forms:**

- **Lots of particles:** the water spreads over many tiny droplets. The clouds are brighter and longer-lasting, and rain less, because small droplets struggle to grow heavy enough to fall.
- **Few particles:** fewer, bigger droplets form. Clouds rain sooner and break up faster.
- **Ice-forming particles:** some biological particles, including certain bacteria and fungal spores, are very good at starting ice crystals. That's often how rain begins, so they can trigger rain.

This is real. Over the Amazon, fungal spores and other biological particles make up a large share of the airborne particles, and the forest partly seeds its own clouds and rain.
