Here's what you proposed, as I understand it. Player text is interpreted into a structured speech act (inform, ask, request, promise, threaten, appeal), with arguments restricted to entities in scope. GOAP, not the interpreter, decides whether anyone is persuaded. NPC output runs the other way: GOAP picks an act, and a realizer turns it into text. Jev would pick the act label from a closed set, and abstaining becomes the in-character "what do you mean?".

**Existing systems, grouped by what they give you**

- **Speech acts as planning operators (the theory):** Allen/Perrault and Cohen/Levesque treated speech acts as plan operators with preconditions and effects, which is nearly your design. Also look at FIPA ACL (agent messaging) and the ISO 24617-2 dialogue act taxonomy for ready-made act vocabularies.
- **Information State Update dialogue managers (TrindiKit, GoDiS):** these keep a structured dialogue state and update it per act.
- **Façade:** free text mapped to discourse acts by hand-built rules, then reaction proposers. Misparses were its weak point.
- **Versu and Prom Week (CiF):** the player picks from a generated action menu, rendered from templates. They are the "buttons first" baseline.
- **Inform 7 / TADS parsers:** text maps to actions, grounded in what's in scope. That is the closest match to your entities-in-scope idea.
- **Grammatical Framework (GF):** one abstract syntax, with parsing and generation both derived from the same grammar. It is the cleanest true bidirectional option. OpenCCG and Prolog DCGs work similarly.
- **Classic NLG pipeline (Reiter and Dale, SimpleNLG):** content selection, sentence planning, and surface realization from a structured message.
- **Cicero (Meta, Diplomacy):** a strategic planner outputs structured intents, and a language model realizes them as text. That is the closest modern analogue to GOAP-chooses, text-realizes.
- **LLMs with constrained decoding (grammar-constrained or JSON schema):** text in gives a schema-valid act, and an act in gives text. Cheap to build, but not interpretable inside.

**How I'd approach it**

1. Define the act schema first: roughly 15 acts, each with typed slots (agent, belief, goal, item).
2. Interpretation has two stages. Cheap candidate generation proposes acts, then Jev picks among them using the NPC's blackboard state. Slots fill only from in-scope entities.
3. Realization starts from templates per act, varied by NPC voice and relationship. A grammar approach like GF gets you reversibility, so what the NPC says is always something the player could say back.
4. Use round-trip testing: realize an act, parse it, and check that you get the same act back. That catches vocabulary drift cheaply.

One idea specific to your setting: since Giglians speak in graded giggles, the realizer could emit act plus intensity as the native output, with human-readable text as a translation layer for the player.

Do you want the parse side and the generate side to share one grammar (GF-style), or is a template-based output with a learned input fine?

Both ideas are sound, and they fit together.

**Subword tokens without an LLM**

You don't need a language model for this, only the tokenizer and embedding part. Options, roughly from simplest to richest:
- **BPE / unigram tokenizer** (SentencePiece style): learns a small vocabulary of frequent fragments, so an unseen word decomposes into known pieces instead of becoming "unknown".
- **fastText-style character n-grams:** a word's vector is the sum of its n-gram vectors. It's tiny, trains fast, and handles typos, inflections, and invented words well. For a game with made-up Giglian vocabulary this is probably the best fit, since a new word like a Giglian term still lands near related ones through shared pieces.
- **Byte-level tokens:** nothing is ever out of vocabulary, at the cost of longer sequences.

Two caveats. Subword splitting makes the input robust, but it does not give you meaning. A foreign word that shares fragments with a known word gets a guessed meaning, which is right for morphology (plural, tense) and wrong for borrowed or coined words. So I'd pair it with an entity linker that matches spans against the in-scope entity names and aliases, which handles proper nouns better than any tokenizer. Also, the game's vocabulary is small and closed, so you can afford to train on exactly your domain.

**Hierarchical interpreter**

This is the stronger idea, because a single pass really does struggle with implicature. A plausible layer stack:
1. **Surface:** tokens to words and entity spans.
2. **Literal content:** a proposition or request frame with slots filled ("it's cold in here" becomes a statement about temperature at a location).
3. **Literal act:** the surface speech act (inform).
4. **Intended act:** the indirect reading (request: close the window).

The step from 3 to 4 is where I'd lean on your GOAP side instead of a learned layer. Indirect speech acts are classically solved by plan recognition: the listener asks what the speaker would want such that saying this makes sense. Your NPCs already maintain a model of the player's likely goals, so that model supplies a top-down prior over intended acts. This is the same shape as predictive coding, which is your research area: lower layers send up literal parses as evidence, and higher layers send down expectations about what the speaker wants. When the two disagree, the NPC asks for clarification or acts on the more likely reading.

Practically, that means the interpreter returns a ranked set of candidate readings per layer instead of one answer, and the NPC's goal model reweights them. It also reuses the same machinery for the NPC's own output in reverse: plan the intended act, then choose a literal form that would let the listener recover it.

**Pushback**

The risk is building a deep stack before you have data showing where one layer fails. I'd test whether layers 1 to 3 in a single classifier handle most player input, and add the top-down layer only for the indirect cases.

How much indirectness do you expect from players, versus mostly direct asks and statements?

The common answer is the **translation convention**: the audience hears English, and the fiction implies it is a rendering of the "real" language. The interesting part is how much of the original shows through.

**Techniques, roughly from least to most visible**

- **Silent translation:** Tolkien frames Westron as rendered into English (Appendix F of LOTR). Rohan's speech becomes Old English to signal "related but foreign," and hobbit place names are translated, while Elvish stays untranslated. Name handling does a lot of the signaling.
- **Subtitle flip:** Dances with Wolves subtitles Lakota but speaks English when it's "the viewer's" language, so which language gets translated tells you whose perspective you hold.
- **Lexical substitution:** Burgess's nadsat (Russian-derived slang inside English) and Gene Wolfe's archaic real words in Book of the New Sun stand in for a world's own vocabulary without a full conlang.
- **Untranslated loanwords for untranslatable concepts:** Dune does this selectively. A few terms per culture, with meaning taught by context, carry flavor.
- **Compositional names:** Dwarf Fortress generates names from a per-language lexicon of concept words and shows the English gloss, so names are decodable. That is nearly the naming scheme I suggested, already proven in a game.
- **Translation as gameplay:** Heaven's Vault, Chants of Sennaar, and Tunic make the language barrier the puzzle. Arrival and Embassytown make the language's structure the story. This only applies if some character truly cannot understand another.
- **Translator device as lampshade:** Mass Effect and Star Trek's universal translators justify English and leave room for mistranslation jokes or untranslatable terms.

**What matters for your case**

Since Giglians share memory through splitting and have no organs, the strongest move is idiom substitution from their own biology. English metaphors that assume hearts, blood, or eyes get replaced by ones that assume pressure, holding, and growth. Players absorb the worldview through phrasing alone, with no vocabulary lessons. A small set of untranslated terms (caste words, legal concepts around split siblings) would cover what English lacks.

This also helps the interpreter. If NPCs consistently use biology-derived idioms, the player learns the dialect, and your realizer templates become the place where that culture lives.

That is very much how Tolkien worked, with one nuance worth knowing: he said (in his letters) that the languages came first and the stories were made to give them a world, the reverse of how it's often told. Either way, the core method is the same, and it's a good one: a language is a record of a people's history, so you build the two as one system.

**What that looks like in practice**

- **Languages are related through events.** Quenya and Sindarin descend from a common ancestor, and the split corresponds to a migration and separation story. Sound change over time and distance does the rest. So you design a proto-language, then let migrations, isolation, and contact decide how the daughters diverge.
- **Names are fossils.** A place called something opaque today was a transparent phrase once, and the drift tells you about the people. Tolkien's names carry layered etymologies, which is why they feel old.
- **Contact leaves marks.** Loanwords show who dominated whom, who traded, and who taught whom. A conquered people's agricultural words survive in the conqueror's speech, while administrative words flow the other way.
- **Prestige and register split.** A ceremonial or scholarly language (Quenya as a Latin-like lore tongue) coexists with the vernacular, and which one a speaker uses signals class and context.
- **Taboo and euphemism rewrite vocabulary.** Politics and religion force words to change, so semantic history is partly a record of what a culture couldn't say plainly.
- **Writing systems spread differently from speech.** Who learned to write, and on what material, shapes which parts of a language fossilize and which keep evolving.

**On "language controls perception"**

The strong version (grammar limits what you can think) is mostly discredited. The weaker versions hold up: habitual categories shift attention and memory, and vocabulary reflects what a culture finds worth distinguishing. For worldbuilding, the dependable direction is culture shaping language (many words for what matters, none for what doesn't), with the reverse showing up as framing and habit. Dramatically, framing effects are plenty.

**A workable loop**

1. Sketch a proto-language and a founding event.
2. Write a short history where groups separate, meet, conquer, trade.
3. Apply sound change per branch, borrow words at contact points, and coin new ones for new institutions.
4. Let the resulting names and idioms feed back into the next chapter of history.

If you want tooling, sound-change appliers exist for conlangers, and a quick search for those will do better than my recollection of specific ones.

The fork you're describing is a known one in philosophy of identity. Parfit's fission cases ask whether you survive a split into two, and his answer was that identity matters less than psychological continuity. Your two options map onto the two sides:

- **Branching:** one experience becomes two, so mitosis is not a death, just a fork. Windows aren't leaseholds that expire, they're leaseholds that sublet.

You don't have to pick, and I'd argue the stronger move is not to. Both halves wake up with real memories and a felt sense of continuity either way, so nothing inside the world can settle it, and the question becomes an unfalsifiable doctrine. That gives you:

- **Schisms:** religious or philosophical factions that disagree on what splitting is, each with its own rites and its own take on the stakes of dying.
- **Political leverage:** a regime's "purge by mitose" reads very differently depending on the view. If splitting is a fork, purging is deletion of a branch. If it's death and birth, every split already ended someone, and the regime is just choosing which heirs count.
- **Uneven splits:** under the death-and-birth view, the half carrying more memory and skill is the heir and the lesser half is closer to a child, which feeds directly into your clean-split elite and the career lottery. Under the branching view, a clean split is a claim to have survived twice.
