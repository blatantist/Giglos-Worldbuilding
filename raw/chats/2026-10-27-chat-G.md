Here's a first-pass sketch. The one structural bet: **speech acts live in the same action library as physical actions**, so there is no separate dialogue system. Everything below hangs off that.

**1. State (per agent, plus shared blackboard)**
- Physical facts, plus *epistemic* facts: beliefs, goals, trust and relationship values, and a model of what other agents believe and want.
- Shared blackboard for group-level facts (threat labels, council alignment).

**2. Action library**
- Physical operators: precondition, effect, cost.
- Verbal operators, one per speech act (inform, ask, request, promise, threaten, appeal, etc.), with typed slots. Their preconditions are epistemic ("speaker believes P", "listener is in earshot"). Their effects are *hypotheses* about the listener's state, since only the listener's own planner decides what actually changes.

**3. Planner**
- Regressive search over the shared library, layered by timescale. High layers plan abstract steps ("get council member X to support Y"), lower layers expand them.
- Verbal steps late in a plan stay abstract and are refined lazily. Only when the plan reaches that step do you pick the concrete act and framing, using the listener's state *at that moment*. Planning exact dialogue ten steps early is wasted work.
- A verbal step's effect carries a confidence, and failure triggers replanning (try a different appeal, or target a different precondition).

**4. Listener-side appraisal**
- A received act is evidence, not a command. The listener runs plan recognition ("why would they say this, what do they want?"), updates beliefs, then checks whether it changes the preconditions of their own goals.
- This is where Jev fits: it picks which precondition or response to pursue from labeled options, including abstain ("what do you mean?").

**5. Interpreter (text in)**
- Surface (tokens, entity linking) → literal content (slot frame) → literal act → intended act.
- The top step is the shared plan-recognition module from #4, so one mechanism serves indirect speech and the emergent threat-labeling.
- Returns ranked candidates, and the NPC either acts on the best one or asks for clarification.

**6. Realizer (text out)**
- Chosen act → template or grammar, varied by NPC voice and relationship.
- Round-trip rule: whatever it says should parse back to the same act.

**7. Execution loop**
- Act, observe the response, reinterpret it through #5, update state, replan if the effect didn't land.

**Questions to settle before we go deeper**
1. Is the player modeled as just another agent in this system (their utterances become acts the NPCs plan against), or special-cased?
2. Do you want to start with the planner/verbal-operator design (#2 and #3), or the interpreter stack (#5)? I'd start with #2, since the act schema constrains everything else.

Fetched and read it. This is Boeda (Square Enix), AIIDE 2021, and it fits our stage 3 almost exactly.

**How it works**
- Every agent runs its own backward GOAP planner, with no commander.
- Conditions are tagged `achievable_by_any_agent` or self-only. "Door is open" is any-agent, "I am not hungry" is self-only.
- If the planner finds no plan, it walks the failed search tree for nodes that contain at least one any-agent condition. At each, it inserts a placeholder **cooperation action** and replans. Any plan that now succeeds contains a cooperation step.
- Messaging then makes that step real: RequestTalk, then RequestCooperation (carrying a list of goal conditions), then a reply, then EndTalk. The receiver checks whether it is busy, and runs its own GOAP to test whether it can achieve those conditions. The requester later confirms completion by perception or a CooperationResult message.

**Its limits, which are the opening for us**
- Discovery is blind. The requester asks whoever it has seen, and the paper admits many failures before one accepts.
- Refusal depends only on busyness and like/dislike. There is no incentive, no negotiation, and no persuasion.
- The request is a bare goal conjunction, with no reasons attached.

**How it maps onto the ladder**
- **Stage 3 is nearly this paper.** Cooperation action plus request, reply, result gives us typed messages. I'd drop RequestTalk and EndTalk, since a handshake only matters for voice.
- **The ring hierarchy fixes blind discovery.** Roles give agents a prior over who can do what, so a farmer asks a hauler, not a random neighbor.
- **Incentives fix refusal.** In our economy the cooperation action carries an *offer*. The receiver's feasibility check becomes "can I do it, and does it serve one of my goals", where a wage is simply a postcondition that satisfies hunger. A job posting is then just a cooperation action published to the board instead of sent to one agent.
- **Stage 4 is the paper's missing half.** When refusal happens, the requester can reason about *which precondition in the receiver's plan* is blocking and answer it. That is persuasion, and it comes straight from the same planner.
- Their CooperationResult is an inform act, so it is already in our schema.

**Suggested first milestone**
Two agents, one chain: a farmer needs hauling to reach the core market and cannot do it alone. Success is the farmer's backward plan failing, the cooperation node being detected, and a hauler accepting an offer. Everything after that is adding rings and agents.

One decision it forces: when the receiver evaluates a request, should it do a full GOAP run (as in the paper, accurate but costly at society scale), or a cheap capability-and-incentive check first with full planning only on accept?

Right, and that is a better framing than the one I gave those stages. The real divide is **reactive** (goals handed in, plan toward them) versus **reflective** (goals justified and checked against predicted futures). Two additions make that concrete.

**1. Goal provenance**
Every goal carries a link to the goal or need it serves: "haul grain" serves "earn coin", which serves "stop being hungry". "Why am I pursuing this?" is then just walking that chain upward. The chain also gives you a cheap test: does this goal still serve its parent under the *predicted* world?

**2. A forward model**
The agent rolls its own state and the abstract world forward (production, stocks, who is likely to do what) and compares the plan's predicted outcome against the parent need. Three things follow:
- A goal can be dropped or swapped *before* its preconditions visibly fail (the road is predicted to be cut, so hauling to that market stops serving "earn coin").
- Prediction error becomes the trigger for reconsideration. That is your predictive-coding framing in practice: stay committed while predictions hold, and replan when error exceeds some precision-weighted threshold.
- Prediction should be coarse and layered by ring and timescale. High layers simulate far ahead on abstract state, and low layers only look a few steps out, which keeps cost sane.

**Why this matters for verbal acts**
It gives language exactly two things to act on:
- **Inform and ask** edit or query the listener's *world model* (so their predictions change).
- **Request with a reason, and persuasion** edit the listener's *goal provenance* ("this serves your hunger better than your current job does").

So the "reason" slot in a request is the provenance link made explicit. That is also what separates your design from the Boeda paper, whose requests carry only a bare goal.

**Revised ladder**
- Stage 3: reactive cooperation (the paper), goals accepted as is.
- Stage 4: goal provenance plus forward model, still no language.
- Stage 5: modeling *other* agents' goals and predicted behavior (plan recognition is this run in reverse).
- Stage 6: verbal acts as interventions on those two structures.
- Stage 7: text interpreter, then politics.

**The ablation to run:** cut a supply link mid-run. Stage 3 agents should keep working toward a dead end until preconditions fail, while stage 4 agents should shift early. The gap in lost output or starvation is your measurable benefit.

One design choice this raises: should agents re-derive their goal from needs every cycle, or commit to a goal and only reconsider on a trigger? I'd lean commitment with trigger-based reconsideration, since it is cheaper and gives you the "stuck branch, give up" behavior you wanted from Jev. Which feels right for your societal model?

**Doctrine as protected nodes**

- Rigidity shouldn't be a ring flag you hand out. Derive it from **how many of the agent's goals hang off the node**, and what dropping it would cost in standing. Inner ring agents have status, privilege, and identity all resting on doctrine, so it is load-bearing for them. Middle ring agents depend on it less, so the same question is cheap to ask. The gradient you described then emerges from the structure.

**Two persuasion routes**
- **Direct:** challenge the protected node. Works on low-rigidity agents, because the forward model's prediction errors ("this isn't serving my goals") face little resistance.
- **Stealth:** never touch the doctrine. Target the *dependent* goals instead, so prediction error accumulates around the node until questioning it becomes cheap. The doctrine is weakened before anyone says anything about it.

**Why stealth works mechanically**
The listener runs plan recognition on you. If your inferred goal conflicts with a protected node, you get labeled. Stealth is therefore *acts whose most likely inferred purpose is benign*. This is the same module as indirect speech interpretation, which is a nice reuse.

**The failure path**
Challenge to a protected node, plus a failed persuasion, yields a threat appraisal. That produces anger (a new goal: neutralize), and a label written to the shared blackboard. The cascade you want comes free, since peers read the label and inherit it. It also explains the earlier tension: loud action raises the label rate, and labels close the inner ring to persuasion.

**The middle ring**
Easier to convince, but also the swing group: they run logistics, and the label cascade can reach them too, so being *seen listening* has a social cost.

**Placement**
A minimal version belongs at stage 4: rigidity values, the walk-up stop, and the anger-and-label response, all without language. Plan recognition arrives at stage 5 and makes stealth meaningful.

**Test:** the same approach on an inner and a middle agent, direct versus indirect. Expected: middle succeeds either way, inner succeeds only indirectly, and inner direct produces a label.

Design question: should rigidity **harden** when challenged (backfire), or stay fixed until prediction error erodes it? Hardening makes failed direct attempts costly later, which strengthens the case for stealth. It also makes the inner ring much harder to move at all, so how hard do you want that to be?

Open design question: is stance something the *speaker chooses* as an independent knob, or is it derived from the speaker's own state (their opinion of the listener, their emotion)? Making it a free knob lets a player be polite while being subversive, whereas deriving it makes characters more consistent but less steerable.

That simplifies the architecture. Reactions, persuasion choices, and clarification can all become **ordinary GOAP actions with confidence-weighted costs**, so there is no separate chooser.

**Where confidence lives**
- **Beliefs:** a confidence per fact, plus trust in the source.
- **Action effects:** especially verbal ones. "If I say this, the listener updates" is a hypothesis with a success confidence, which is your model of the listener.
- **Provenance links:** how confident am I that this goal still serves its parent need?

**What it does to planning**
- Plan confidence is the product of step confidences, so long plans are discounted automatically. That makes lazy refinement and hierarchy natural: abstract steps are vague and carry lower confidence, so you commit to them cheaply and sharpen later.
- **Information actions come free.** Ask, scout, and verify raise the confidence of downstream steps, so the planner picks them whenever the gain beats their cost. Clarifying a murky player utterance ("what do you mean?") falls out of the same rule, chosen when interpretation confidence is low and a misread is expensive.
- **Reactions** (update, rebut, disengage, confront, label) are actions whose costs are shifted by the appraisal: content threat and stance modify them. Contempt raises the cost of engaging and lowers the cost of excluding, with no scorer involved.

**Doctrine falls out neatly**
A protected node is a belief with very high confidence *and* high resistance to revision. Predictive coding gives the update rule: the shift equals prediction error, weighted by how precise the prior is relative to the evidence. Hardening under attack is then a precision increase.

**Two cautions**
1. **One scalar conflates two things.** "How likely is this true" and "how hard is this to move" are different. A new rumor can be 50% likely and trivially revisable, while doctrine is 99% and nearly immovable. I'd keep them as two values per belief: probability and precision.
2. **Stuck branches.** Part of Jev's appeal was letting an agent *abandon* a failing branch instead of following the highest score. Pure confidence can loop on a dead end. The fix is that each failed attempt lowers the relevant confidence, and plans below a commitment threshold trigger reconsideration, with persistence as a per-agent trait.

Also, hand-set confidence numbers are the tuning burden. Starting with three coarse levels (low, medium, high) keeps behavior legible while you learn what matters.

Do you want probability and precision as separate values from the start, or begin with one scalar and split once you hit a case that needs it?

- **The act carries a small paralinguistic vector** (computed from the speaker's state, always, for every agent).

- **Listeners read the same vector directly**, as agents, without needing audio.

**What goes in the vector**
Keep it low-dimensional and tied to things the sim already tracks:
- Confidence (from belief probability), and certainty (from precision)
- Valence and arousal
- Stance (respect versus contempt, the modifier from earlier)
- Focus: which slot of the act is the new or important information, which drives emphasis

Text hedges ("I think", "definitely") derive from the same confidence value, so text and voice never disagree.

- **Leakage as a mechanic.** If the vector is derived from true internal state, voices betray beliefs. A liar's stated content and leaked confidence diverge, and anyone sensitive to the channel can catch it, including NPCs. That is your "player infers beliefs better" goal as a game system.

- **Speaking is an action.** Its effect is an event in the world: an act plus its paralinguistic vector, emitted from a position with a loudness.
- **Hearing is perception.** Every agent within the signal's footprint receives it, degraded by distance and noise. No session, no "talk request".
- **Each listener independently** interprets it, updates beliefs, and lets it change which goals are salient. If a heard act makes a goal worth pursuing (answering, helping, avoiding, reporting it), the planner picks it like any other action.

**What falls out for free**
- **Eavesdropping and the player overhearing:** anything in range hears it.
- **Interruptions and overlap:** two signals can arrive at once, and a loud one can mask a quiet one.
- **The player as just another source:** their voice or text enters as the same kind of event.
- **Conversation ending:** nothing sends EndTalk. It stops when no listener has a goal that a reply would advance, and salience decays.

**What replaces the handshake**
1. **Addressing.** Getting attention is itself a cheap act (a name, a gesture, a call), and orientation and proximity are evidence of who is being spoken to. Each listener estimates "was that for me?" with a confidence, which fits your confidence-based design.
2. **Dialogue context** lives in each agent's own beliefs: a short, decaying record of recently heard acts and who sent them. Two agents can therefore disagree about what was just said, which is realistic and a good source of misunderstanding.
3. **Turn-taking** comes from action duration. Speaking takes time, and a reply has latency tied to the listener's planning and cost, so a hesitant or low-confidence agent answers more slowly.

**The failure mode to design for**
Courtesy loops. If "thanks" triggers "you're welcome" triggers "no problem", you get endless chatter. **One decision:** should the addressee be an **explicit slot in the act**, or purely inferred by listeners from gaze, position, and naming? Explicit slots make the interpreter simpler and debugging easier, while inference is more faithful to your emergent model, and mistakes ("I thought you meant me") become a real behavior. Which do you want in the first version?

Yes, with no new mechanism. It is the same pipeline as agent-to-agent persuasion, with the player as the speaker.

**The flow**
1. The player's request and explanation arrive as events, which every nearby agent hears.
2. The interpreter turns them into a goal state (what the player wants true) plus a reason, if one was given.
3. The listener appraises it: can I plan toward it (feasibility), does it conflict with a protected belief, how much do I trust this speaker, and what stance did it carry?
4. If the net value is positive, they **adopt the goal**, and the planner takes over from there like any other goal.
